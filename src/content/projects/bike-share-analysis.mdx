## Overview

How do casual riders and annual members use bike-share services differently? This case study uses real Divvy trip data to explore that question within the fictional Cyclistic marketing scenario.

## The Challenge

Twelve monthly CSV files contain millions of trips. Before comparing rider groups, the analysis must reconcile missing station information, duplicates, geographic outliers and invalid trip durations.

## Approach & Development

The workflow follows six phases: Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyze, Share and Act. R’s data.table package reads the monthly files, while tidyr, dplyr and readr support preparation and analysis.

The cleaning stage checks unique ride identifiers, filters geographic anomalies and invalid duration values, and creates time-based attributes for aggregation. It retains records with missing station fields because other attributes remain useful; station-level findings must account for those gaps.

After cleaning, **5,473,753 trips** remain. The notebook records **459,959 removed rows**. Tableau visualisations compare the two rider groups by weekday, month, hour and bicycle type.

## Key Challenges & Solutions

### Missing stations without losing useful trips

More than a quarter of the source records have missing station information. Deleting all of them would remove useful timing and membership information, so the analysis keeps them and notes the limitation for relevant comparisons.

### Distinguishing usage from preference

Bike-type counts alone cannot establish user preference: the data does not describe the supply or geographic distribution of each type. The case study treats that comparison as exploratory.

## Final Result

Members account for approximately **66%** of analysed trips and casual riders for **34%**. Members show stronger weekday use and peaks around 08:00 and 17:00, while casual usage is more concentrated on weekends. Both groups show pronounced seasonal variation.

These patterns support the notebook’s proposed focus on weekend campaigns and clearer communication of membership benefits. They are recommendations from a case study, not measured campaign outcomes.
